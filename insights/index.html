01 / CLOUD & PLATFORMS

Cloud readiness begins with the things you cannot see.

A migration plan is only as useful as its understanding of the system that already exists.

Read the full cloud readiness article

It is tempting to begin a cloud conversation with a destination: a platform, a target architecture, or a date. But a working application is more than its servers. It also includes a overnight file transfer, an undocumented account, and the person who knows what to restart when a queue stops moving. Those dependencies deserve a place in the plan.

Map the journey of one transaction

Choose a meaningful user task and follow it from start to finish. Record the systems it touches, the information it carries, and the teams that support it. Ask what happens if each dependency is slow or unavailable. This makes an abstract inventory into a picture of business continuity.

Make ownership part of readiness

For each workload, identify who can approve a change, who receives an alert, and who can restore service. Check whether backups have actually been restored in a test. Discuss acceptable downtime and data loss with the people affected, rather than treating those choices as infrastructure defaults.

Choose a first move that teaches you something

A useful pilot is bounded and reversible, but representative enough to expose real constraints. Agree measures before it begins: recovery time, delivery effort, operating cost, and user-visible performance. Include the cost of running old and new environments together, not only the eventual hosting bill.

A readiness assessment should end with decisions, not just diagrams. What can move now? What needs redesign? What is better left alone? The value lies in making those trade-offs visible while there is still time to change direction.

Try this: ask two people to independently draw the same application's dependencies. Investigate the differences before choosing a migration date.

02 / DATA & APPLIED AI

The most useful part of an AI system may be its boundary.

Defining what a tool must not do can be more valuable than extending what it can generate.

Read the full responsible AI article

A fluent answer creates an impression of competence. That impression can conceal a missing source, a mistaken assumption, or an instruction hidden in an untrusted document. A responsible AI design begins by separating an impressive demonstration from evidence that a system is suitable for a particular task.

Write the task in ordinary language

Replace a broad ambition such as "automate support" with a bounded task: "draft a response using approved guidance for a person to review." State which information may be used, who will see the output, and which decisions stay with a human. If the team cannot describe a safe failure, the scope needs more work.

Evaluate the whole interaction

Accuracy alone is too narrow. Can a user verify the answer? Can they see when evidence is incomplete? Does the interface invite correction, or does it make acceptance the easiest path? Include ambiguous questions, outdated sources, unsupported requests, and attempts to override the instructions in the test set.

Compare results with a simpler alternative, such as search or a well-designed form. AI is only a useful choice when its benefits justify the additional uncertainty, operating cost, and oversight. Record the failures as carefully as the successes.

Give someone the authority to stop

A pilot needs an owner, an escalation route, and a way to withdraw the feature if its behaviour becomes unacceptable. Updates to source data, prompts, permissions, or models can alter outcomes, so evaluation should recur rather than conclude with a launch checklist.

Boundaries do not make a system safe by themselves. They make expectations testable and accountability clearer. That is a stronger starting point than asking people to trust a tool because it sounds confident.

Try this: write three situations where your proposed assistant should decline, ask for clarification, or hand the task to a person. Make them acceptance tests.

03 / STRATEGY & EXPERIENCE

Good design leaves room for a different way through.

A digital journey becomes stronger when it works beyond the most convenient assumptions.

Read the full design article

Many interfaces are designed around an ideal moment: a large screen, a fast connection, a confident user, and no interruptions. Real life supplies a different brief. Someone may be using a keyboard, enlarging the text, managing fatigue, or returning to a task after a long pause. These are ordinary conditions, not exceptions to design around later.

Start with the decision, not the screen

Ask what the person needs to understand and what they need to do next. Give actions specific labels. Explain consequences before asking for commitment. A clear heading and a well-placed sentence can remove more friction than an elaborate interaction.

Build more than one reliable route

Use semantic controls so keyboard and assistive technology users can reach the same actions. Keep focus visible. Do not rely on colour alone to communicate status. Test enlarged text and narrow viewports with real content, including long names and helpful error messages.

Progressive disclosure can make a page easier to scan without hiding essential context. A summary should describe what opening it reveals. Important limitations, prices, or submission consequences should remain visible before the user commits to an action.

Treat recovery as part of the journey

A form should explain which field needs attention and how to correct it. A confirmation should say what actually happened, not merely display a celebratory icon. If information was only saved as a local draft, say so. Honest feedback gives people the confidence to continue or stop.

Accessible design is not a final polish or a promise that one test can prove. It is an ongoing practice of reducing assumptions, observing real use, and correcting the barriers that remain.

Try this: complete the most important journey with only a keyboard, then repeat at a narrow viewport with enlarged text. Note where understanding or control is lost.
